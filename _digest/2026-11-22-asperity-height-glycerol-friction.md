## What's new

The authors ran nonequilibrium molecular dynamics simulations of glycerol, a high-viscosity lubricant, squeezed between a hydroxylated SiO2 surface and a CH-terminated (non-polar) surface. They varied the height of surface asperities (bumps) from 0 to 6.4 Å and the film thickness from 2 to 8 molecular layers.

On the non-polar surface with low roughness (asperity height up to 3.2 Å), the film slips at the wall. At 4.8 Å a mixed mode appears that combines wall slip with shear inside the lubricant layers. The authors infer the transition lies between the tested heights of 3.2 and 4.8 Å. Across the range, friction coefficient and heat dissipation rate rise by more than two orders of magnitude as asperity height goes from 0 to 6.4 Å. Force analysis traces the extra resistance mainly to a "wedge effect" and to lubricant piling up on the uphill side of the bumps.

## Why it matters

This work indicates that atomic-scale roughness can decide how energy is dissipated in a molecularly thin film. For anyone modelling boundary lubrication, it is a concrete case where the slip mode, not just viscosity, sets friction. The suggested design rule is to keep asperities low so the film slips as a 2D-like layer, which is relevant to superlubricity.

## Caveats

The results come from simulations of one lubricant, glycerol, on two idealized surface types, with no experiments reported in the abstract. Only discrete asperity heights were tested, so the exact transition height is not pinned down. Real contacts have more varied roughness, additives, and sliding conditions than these model systems.
